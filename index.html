History & Origins

Can You Cross Every Bridge

Before you read any further, try this yourself. The city of Konigsberg had four landmasses (A, B, C, D) linked by seven bridges. The challenge residents set themselves was simple: walk through the city crossing every single bridge exactly once. Click a landmass to start your walk, then keep clicking landmasses to cross bridges between them. Give it a real try, several different starting points, before reading on.

Click a landmass (A, B, C, or D) to begin your walk.

Bridges crossed: 0 / 7

Stuck? A mathematician named Leonhard Euler faced this exact same challenge in 1735.

The city of Königsberg (in the Kingdom of Prussia; today Kaliningrad, Russia) was built around the river Pregel, which split the city into four separate landmasses: two riverbanks and two islands. Seven bridges connected these four landmasses to one another. A popular pastime among residents was to try to find a walking route that crossed every one of the seven bridges exactly once, no bridge skipped, none crossed twice.

The Swiss mathematician Leonhard Euler (1707, 1783) heard of the puzzle and, rather than trying every possible route by trial and error, found a way to prove it was impossible in general. He presented his solution to the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences in 1735, and it was published in 1736 under the title:

Leonhard Euler (1736). "Solutio problematis ad geometriam situs pertinentis" ("The solution of a problem relating to the geometry of position"). Commentarii Academiae Scientiarum Petropolitanae, Vol. 8, pp. 128 to 140.

The phrase geometria situs ("geometry of position") had been used decades earlier by Leibniz to describe a hoped-for geometry concerned with position and relationship rather than measured distances and angles. Euler's paper is the first time that idea was actually carried out on a concrete problem, by discarding the exact shapes, sizes, and distances of Königsberg entirely, and keeping only which landmasses were joined to which, and how many times. That reduction, four points, seven connecting lines, is recognised today as the first graph ever analysed mathematically, and Euler's paper as the founding document of graph theory (the words "graph," "vertex," and "edge" came later; Euler himself wrote in terms of regions and crossings).

Euler's argument still holds up perfectly today, and it explains exactly why your attempt above kept failing. Every time you enter a landmass by one bridge, you must leave it by a different one, so any landmass you pass through (without starting or ending there) needs its bridges to come in pairs, i.e. an even number of bridges. Only a landmass where you start or end your walk is allowed to have an odd number. So a walk that uses every bridge exactly once can only exist if the graph has zero or exactly two landmasses with an odd number of bridges (an odd degree, in modern terms).

In Königsberg, every single landmass had an odd number of bridges:

  • Kneiphof island (A): 5 bridges
  • East island / Lomse (B): 3 bridges
  • West bank (C): 3 bridges
  • East bank (D): 3 bridges

Four odd-degree landmasses, two more than the rule allows, so Euler proved no such walk could ever exist, for any starting point or route. No matter how many times you tried above, or which landmass you started from, you were always going to get stuck. This result is now known as Euler's theorem for Eulerian paths/circuits, and it's the same test used today to check whether a route (a mail delivery round, a street-cleaning path, a circuit-board trace) can cover every connection exactly once.

Königsberg still exists today, it's the Russian city of Kaliningrad. Only five of the original seven bridges survive; two were destroyed in the Second World War. Even with a different bridge layout, mathematicians have checked: an Eulerian walk still isn't possible today either.

Why It Matters

Graphs in Real Life & in Tech

The same "dots and lines" idea Euler used for bridges now underlies huge parts of science and technology. Each card below shows the same example two ways: first the abstract graph, then what it actually looks like in real life.

Computer Science

Computer Networks & the Internet

Every computer, router, and server online is a vertex; every cable or wireless link between them is an edge. Routing algorithms decide how to get a data packet from one vertex to another by searching this graph for a path.

Example: when you load a webpage, your request may hop across a dozen routers, each hop is one edge in a giant global graph.

Chemistry / STEM

Molecular Structure

Chemists draw molecules exactly like graphs: atoms are vertices, chemical bonds are edges. Ring-shaped molecules like benzene are literally cycle graphs, and the degree of an atom's vertex reflects how many bonds it forms.

Example: benzene (C₆H₆)'s six carbon atoms form a 6-cycle, each carbon is 2-regular within the ring.

Navigation / STEM

Maps & GPS Navigation

Road maps are graphs where intersections are vertices and roads are edges, each labelled with a weight (distance or travel time). GPS apps use shortest-path algorithms over this weighted graph to pick your route.

Example: "fastest route" and "shortest route" can differ because they minimise different edge weights on the same graph.

Software Engineering

Task Scheduling & Dependencies

Build tools, project plans, and compilers represent tasks as vertices and must-happen-before rules as directed edges. The graph must have no cycles (a DAG, directed acyclic graph) or the tasks could never be scheduled at all.

Example: a compiler won't build file B before file A if B depends on A, that dependency is one directed edge.

Real-Life Example

Argentina 2–1 England, 2026 World Cup Semifinal

A broadcast DAG in the wild: a loose ball falls to Messi, who crosses to Lautaro Martínez, who heads it in for the stoppage-time winner. Watch each edge draw in as the ball arrives and fade once play has moved past it, possession only ever flows forward, never back, which is exactly what makes this a directed acyclic graph.

Example: GOAL has in-degree 1 and out-degree 0, a pendant vertex reached by a single pendant edge from Lautaro.

A few more places the same idea shows up:

  • Search engines treat the web as a directed graph of pages linked by hyperlinks, Google's original PageRank algorithm essentially measures how "important" a vertex is based on the graph's structure.
  • Social networks represent people as vertices and friendships/follows as edges, see the interactive example below.
  • Electrical circuits and logistics/supply chains are modelled as graphs to analyse current flow and shipping routes respectively.

Additional Resources

Go Deeper: Articles & Videos

This module covers the essentials, but graph theory goes much further. Here's a short, curated list, no filler, for anyone who wants to read or watch more, roughly ordered from "quick overview" to "full course."

Articles & Reference

  • Graph TheoryWikipedia. The best single overview: definitions, history, and links out to every subfield (coloring, planarity, algorithms, and more).
  • Graph TheoryEncyclopaedia Britannica. A concise, carefully edited overview of graphs, degree, paths, and connectivity, good for a fast, reliable refresher.
  • Mathematics for Computer Science, Ch. 5: Graph TheoryMIT OpenCourseWare (free PDF). Rigorous but readable lecture notes covering exactly this module's ideas, order, degree, subgraphs, with full proofs.
  • Introduction to Graph Data StructureGeeksforGeeks. A fast, example-heavy reference aimed at programmers, good for connecting these definitions to actual code.
  • Describing GraphsKhan Academy. Part of a free interactive course on graph representations and algorithms, with quizzes built in.
  • Graph TheoryBrilliant.org Wiki. Short, visual explanations with worked practice problems archived

Videos

  • Graph Theory on NumberphileNumberphile (10-video playlist). Bite-sized, entertaining explainers of Euler's bridge problem and other famous graph puzzles, a great starting point.
  • Graph Theory seriesDr. Sarada Herke. A full university-level course covering, in order, almost every term in this module: definitions, families of graphs, degree, connectivity, subgraphs, and bipartite graphs.
  • Graph Theory AlgorithmsWilliam Fiset, via freeCodeCamp (7-hour course). For going beyond definitions into real algorithms, BFS/DFS, shortest paths, spanning trees, with working code.
  • Lecture 6: Graph Theory and ColoringMIT OpenCourseWare. A full university lecture that builds the same vocabulary from scratch, then applies it to scheduling and coloring problems.
